The big question

Why did people living in separate German and Italian states want to become part of one nation?

In the early 1800s, Germany and Italy were not unified countries. People lived in separate kingdoms, duchies, and states. Some regions were influenced or controlled by foreign powers. During the 19th century, nationalist movements increasingly challenged this political division.

Your mission: Investigate the reasons for unification. Focus on ideas, motivations, and consequences—not memorizing every leader or battle. Open the resources in a new tab and return here to record your answers.

Part A · Before and after: from divided states to nations /6

Compare the maps of Germany and Italy before and after unification. Look for the many separate kingdoms and states in 1815, the larger united countries that emerged by 1870–1871, and their present-day borders. You do not need to study a timeline.

The 1815 and 1870–1871 maps are simplified classroom schematics, not exact historical boundary maps. The modern maps use present-day geographic outlines. They show how numerous states became unified countries. The German Empire continued to contain constituent states, and ethnic and linguistic diversity existed in both regions.

Key idea — ethnic nationalism: People may see themselves as part of the same nation because they share features such as language, cultural traditions, history, or ancestry, even if they live under different governments. This does not mean everyone in a region had the same identity or supported unification.
